About the project

HERON

Hydrological Estimation via Reflectometry from an Overflying Navigation-signal Receiver
The idea

Satellite signals bounce, and water is a mirror

Navigation satellites send radio signals at about 1.2 and 1.6 GHz. A small part of each signal reflects off the ground near the specular point, where the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Calm water reflects these signals much more strongly and more coherently than dry soil or vegetation.

From orbit, missions such as CYGNSS use this effect over large areas. From a drone flying tens of metres up, the reflecting area is small, so the map can resolve small ponds, wetlands and flooded fields. Confirm the planned flight altitude and the target features.

The aircraft

A drone that listens up and down

An up-looking right-hand circularly polarised (RHCP) antenna receives the direct signal. A down-looking antenna receives the reflection in both RHCP and LHCP; comparing the two polarisations helps tell smooth water from rough or vegetated ground. The antenna-to-channel map is still a draft in PAYLOAD_ARCHITECTURE.md.

The payload uses the Cube autopilot's telemetry radio to talk to the ground: HERON messages travel inside MAVLink TUNNEL messages. It does not depend on the flight controller's state, and it keeps recording if the link drops.

The payload

Six coherent channels on one clock

Two Ettus B210 and two Ettus B200 software-defined radios record six channels of raw radio samples. A HERON-designed clock board gives all four radios the same 10 MHz reference (from a ±0.5 ppm temperature-compensated oscillator) and the same pulse-per-second, so every channel starts on the same edge and stays coherent.

An Intel NUC runs the recorders and writes 8-bit complex samples to a dedicated SSD. Nothing is processed in the air: all the science happens on the ground, after the flight.

The signals

Seven signals, three constellations, two bands

One recorded channel per band holds every signal in that band; the processing separates them by their codes. The faster L5-band codes resolve shorter path differences, which suits the short reflected paths of a low-flying drone. The signal plan part of the course explains why.

On the ground

Plan, fly, monitor, process

The mission planner places the drone so that the reflections land on the water, exports the flight plan, and monitors the aircraft, the payload and the RTK base station during the flight. Try it in your browser.

A GNSS base station gives correction data, so the drone position is known to centimetre level (RTK and PPK). After the flight, the processing software acquires and tracks each satellite, measures the reflected power, and maps each reflection to its point on the ground.
