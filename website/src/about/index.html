About the project

HERON

A drone payload that records direct and reflected GNSS signals, and the ground software that turns those recordings into a map of surface water. Expand the acronym HERON here if it has one.

The idea: GNSS reflectometry

Navigation satellites send radio signals at about 1.2 and 1.6 GHz. A small part of each signal reflects off the ground near the specular point, the place where the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Calm water is a good mirror at these frequencies, so a reflection from water is much stronger and more coherent than a reflection from dry soil or vegetation.

From orbit, missions such as CYGNSS use this effect over large areas. From a drone flying tens of metres up, the reflecting area is small, so the map can show small ponds, wetlands and flooded fields. Confirm the planned flight altitude and the target features.

What the system does

  • Record in flight. The payload records raw radio samples (IQ) from the direct and the reflected antennas. It does no science processing in the air.
  • Monitor from the ground. A ground station laptop shows live health over the drone's telemetry radio, and starts and stops the recording. If the link drops, the payload keeps recording. Try the ground station simulation.
  • Position precisely. A GNSS base station on the ground gives correction data, so the drone position is known to centimetre level (RTK and PPK).
  • Process after the flight. Ground software acquires and tracks each satellite in the direct recording, measures the reflected power, and maps each reflection to its specular point.

The payload

Six coherent channels

Two channels record the direct signal from an up-looking right-hand circularly polarised (RHCP) antenna, one for each frequency band. Four channels record the reflected signal from a down-looking antenna in both RHCP and LHCP, for each band. Comparing the two polarisations helps tell smooth water from rough or vegetated ground. The antenna-to-channel map is still a draft in PAYLOAD_ARCHITECTURE.md.

Four radios, one clock

Two Ettus B210 and two Ettus B200 software-defined radios make the six channels. A HERON-designed clock board gives all four the same 10 MHz reference (from a ±0.5 ppm temperature-compensated oscillator) and the same one-pulse-per-second signal, so all channels start on the same edge and stay coherent with each other.

A small computer

An Intel NUC (NUC7i3DNB) runs the recorders and the payload supervisor. It writes 8-bit complex samples to a dedicated SATA SSD, with the operating system on a separate NVMe drive.

Talks over the autopilot's radio

The payload does not depend on the flight controller's state, but it uses the Cube autopilot's telemetry link as its radio: HERON messages travel inside MAVLink TUNNEL messages to the ground station.

The signals

HERON records seven open-service signals from three constellations, on two carrier frequencies. One recorded channel per band holds every signal in that band; the processing separates them by their codes.

The faster L5-band codes resolve shorter path differences, which suits the short reflected paths of a low-flying drone. The signal plan part of the course explains why.
